## 1. Purpose and output selection

Convert a complete CPO analysis into a compact visual decision system. Distillation must remove repetition, not evidence, trade-offs, economics, or uncertainty.

Choose the smallest complete artifact:

| Decision need | Default artifact |
|---|---|
| Understand one strategy at a glance | Strategy-on-a-page |
| Approve one consequential choice | Executive decision one-pager |
| Defend a recommendation and discuss it live | Short strategic presentation |
| Compare futures and contingent moves | Scenario map plus signposts |
| Reallocate across products or bets | Portfolio map plus allocation table |
| Explain value and profit mechanism | Unit-economics or value-driver tree |
| Align strategy with execution | Outcome roadmap or strategy-to-delivery flow |
| Show causal metric logic | Metric tree with guardrails |

If the user does not specify a format, place a strategy-on-a-page or compact executive visual summary before the detailed analysis when the work spans several strategic dimensions. Keep a narrow answer narrow.

Do not create a presentation merely because the analysis is long. Create one when the audience, decision, or live discussion benefits from a staged narrative.

## 2. Visual reasoning rules

Build the communication layer after the analysis is coherent.

1. Start with the audience's decision, not the framework used.
2. Use a conclusion headline: `Narrow to regulated mid-market` rather than `Target segment`.
3. Give each page or slide one primary message and one clear reading order.
4. Show the mechanism connecting customer value, strategic advantage, economics, and action.
5. Make exclusions visible. A strategy without `not now` or `will not do` is incomplete.
6. Put evidence, assumptions, and unknowns next to the claim they qualify.
7. Use diagrams only for genuine relationships; use tables for exact comparisons and charts for quantitative patterns.
8. Prefer direct labels to legends and decorative color coding.
9. Use visual emphasis for decision importance, not decoration.
10. Keep detailed calculations, source notes, and secondary analysis in an appendix or supporting artifact.

Apply a strict density budget:

- one headline conclusion per page or slide;
- one dominant visual, comparison, or evidence object;
- at most three short supporting annotations and 20–45 visible words on a normal slide;
- four to six semantic zones and no more than 250 visible words on a one-pager;
- no prose paragraph as the main content of a visual artifact;
- one compact diagram or one compact table on a one-pager, not both;
- move formulas, detailed test plans, source ledgers, and secondary risk registers to supporting material.

Treat these word budgets as maximum-density guardrails, not targets. Count all audience-visible titles, labels, annotations, table cells, and footnotes before delivery. Exclude speaker notes and appendix slides from the primary-slide count. A genuinely necessary comparison-table slide may use up to 90 visible words; do not permit other exceptions or a return to report-like pages.

If content does not fit at a readable size, cut, combine, sequence, or move it to an appendix. Never solve density by shrinking type or adding more boxes.

Avoid:

- dense prose arranged inside boxes;
- a grid of many equal-weight cards with no hierarchy;
- framework logos and ornamental canvases;
- feature inventories masquerading as roadmaps;
- unlabeled axes or unsupported 2x2 placement;
- fabricated charts, market sizes, forecasts, or precision;
- traffic-light status without an explicit rule for each color;
- tiny source notes or critical caveats hidden in footers.

## 3. Strategy-on-a-page

Use for orientation and alignment around one coherent strategy. Build a single visual field with these semantic zones:

### Decision spine

- **Objective and horizon:** measurable outcome when supported; otherwise the decision objective and review horizon.
- **Diagnosis:** pivotal challenge, change, or constraint.
- **Chosen arena:** target segment/job/context and explicit non-target.
- **Winning logic:** differentiated value and advantage mechanism.
- **Guiding policy:** the governing choice that coordinates action.

### Execution spine

- **Coherent bets:** usually three to five, each linked to the guiding policy.
- **Economic mechanism:** how the bets change willingness to pay, contribution, retention, cost, capacity, or mission value.
- **Measures:** outcome, leading indicators, and guardrails.
- **Evidence gates:** material assumptions, cheapest credible tests, and change/stop conditions.
- **Non-goals:** work intentionally excluded or deferred.

Use arrows only when they communicate real dependency or causality. A useful reading path is:

`Diagnosis → choice → advantage → bets → outcomes/economics → evidence gates`

Choose one primary composition for the page. Do not stack a flowchart, options table, detailed economics formula, risk register, and action plan on the same page. Compress secondary layers to a label plus one decision-relevant line and keep their full versions outside the one-pager. End the one-pager after these zones; do not append the underlying report.

The one-page view must answer without supplementary narration:

- What did we decide?
- Why this and not the alternatives?
- How should it create customer and business value?
- What will we do and stop doing?
- What evidence could change the strategy?

## 4. Executive decision one-pager

Use when an executive, founder, investment committee, or board must approve, reject, fund, sequence, or stop something.

Place the decision and recommendation at the top. Use the remaining space for:

1. **Why now:** decision trigger and cost of delay or premature commitment.
2. **Options:** materially different choices, including do-nothing when viable.
3. **Recommendation:** chosen option and decisive reason.
4. **Economics/value:** dominant drivers, break-even logic, or missing inputs.
5. **Risks:** load-bearing assumptions, reversibility, and failure modes.
6. **Decision gates:** next evidence, owner, and what result changes the choice.
7. **Ask:** the exact approval, resource, access, or next decision required.

Use a comparison table when option details must remain exact. Do not compress an entire strategy into the decision one-pager; link or append the supporting analysis.

## 5. Strategic presentation

Use the shortest complete narrative. A typical decision deck may include:

1. decision and recommendation;
2. what changed and why the decision exists now;
3. diagnosis and evidence;
4. strategic options and trade-offs;
5. chosen strategy and winning mechanism;
6. economics and scenarios;
7. bets, evidence gates, and outcome roadmap;
8. risks, non-goals, and explicit ask.

Omit or combine slides when a shorter story remains complete. Add slides only for a real audience question, decision dependency, or evidence layer.

For every slide:

- write the title as the answer or claim;
- identify the audience question it resolves;
- choose one dominant evidence object: chart, table, diagram, number, quote, or option comparison;
- add only the minimum explanation needed to interpret it;
- end with an implication when the action is not obvious.

Write actual on-slide copy, not report paragraphs or speaker notes. A normal slide should contain a claim title, one dominant object, and up to three brief annotations totaling no more than 45 visible words. A comparison slide may use up to 90 visible words only when a compact table is the dominant object; keep it to the minimum rows and columns needed for the decision. If the reasoning needs more words, add a second slide or put the detail in speaker notes or the appendix.

When the user requests deck structure without a file, specify each slide as `claim title + visual/object + on-slide annotations`; do not write a paragraph that would be pasted onto the slide.

Do not transfer report section names directly into slide titles. `Market`, `Customers`, and `Roadmap` are topics, not messages.

Keep appendices available for definitions, detailed economics, research evidence, alternative scenarios, methodology, and source traceability. Do not use the appendix to hide a fact that reverses the recommendation.

## 6. Scenario, portfolio, economics, and roadmap visuals

### Scenario map

Show:

- two or more decision-relevant futures driven by explicit uncertainties;
- the causal story of each scenario;
- leading signposts that distinguish them;
- no-regret moves, options to preserve, and contingent commitments.

Do not assign probabilities without evidence. A 2x2 is optional, not mandatory; use a table or branching map if it communicates assumptions better.

### Portfolio map

Show each product or bet's strategic role, lifecycle, evidence of pull, economic contribution or mission value, capacity demand, risk, and recommended posture. Use a 2x2 only as an orientation layer, never as the sole decision rule. Accompany it with allocation or transition decisions.

### Economics visual

Choose the form that matches the decision:

- value-driver tree for causal economic logic;
- waterfall for movement between revenue and contribution states;
- cohort chart for retention, expansion, or payback behavior;
- sensitivity or tornado chart for dominant uncertainty;
- scenario table for transparent assumptions and outputs;
- break-even curve when the decision depends on volume, price, utilization, or cost.

Every quantitative visual must show units, period, population, source or assumption status, and the applicable contribution boundary.

### Outcome roadmap

Show outcomes and evidence gates before features. Use `Now / Next / Later` only if those horizons are meaningful; otherwise use decision or review points. Connect each bet to an outcome, metric, owner, dependency, and stop/change trigger. Avoid unsupported dates.

### Metric tree

Separate the outcome or North Star, controllable inputs, state transitions, and guardrails. Mark hypotheses where causal links are not established. Keep metric definitions outside the visual when they would overload it, but retain the measurement contract in the supporting material.

## 7. Audience adaptation

Adapt emphasis without changing the evidence:

| Audience | Emphasize | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Founder/CEO | choice, speed, opportunity cost, economics, irreversible risks | functional detail without decision impact |
| Board/investor | portfolio logic, trajectory, capital/resource implications, scenarios, governance asks | feature inventory and delivery theater |
| Product/engineering/design | outcomes, customer problem, assumptions, dependencies, guardrails, decision rights | strategy slogans without operational meaning |
| Sales/marketing/customer success | target, positioning, qualification, promise boundaries, feedback loop | internal framework language |
| Cross-functional leadership | trade-offs, allocation, ownership, cadence, escalation rules | ambiguous shared accountability |

When one artifact must serve several audiences, optimize the main page or deck for the decision owner and move function-specific detail to appendices or companion views.

## 8. Evidence and uncertainty

Preserve the evidence ledger visually:

- **Verified:** solid treatment and cited source;
- **Inference:** visibly labeled interpretation;
- **Assumption:** hypothesis marker and planned test;
- **Estimate:** range plus input provenance;
- **Unknown:** empty state plus the decision it blocks.

Do not use color alone to encode these states. Add text labels, icons with a key, line styles, or annotations that remain intelligible in grayscale.

For external research, cite material claims on the page or slide where they appear. For internal data, name the source, snapshot date, population, and metric definition. If provenance would overwhelm the visual, use compact source markers linked to a readable source appendix.

If a number is unavailable, visualize the formula, threshold relationship, or data gap. Never replace a missing input with a decorative placeholder value.

## 9. Artifact production and QA

When the user requests an editable presentation or one-page slide, use the `Presentations` skill and follow all of its narrative, implementation, rendering, font, overlap, and visual-QA requirements. Deliver the actual `.pptx`, not only a textual outline.

When the user requests a distributable single-page PDF, use the appropriate document or PDF creation skill and verify the rendered page. When format is unspecified and a file would materially improve communication, prefer an editable one-page slide; state the choice briefly.

For a lightweight in-chat view, use a compact table, Mermaid diagram, or other supported visualization only if it preserves the intended relationship. Do not use a large Markdown heading-and-bullet document as a substitute for visual design.

When delivering an in-chat one-pager, show the compact visual first and limit accompanying prose to essential caveats or the next decision. Do not follow it automatically with the full underlying report.

Before production, confirm or infer:

- decision owner and audience;
- setting: asynchronous reading, live presentation, workshop, or board pack;
- required file format and editability;
- brand/template constraints;
- data and source availability;
- confidentiality or redaction needs.

Ask only when a missing answer materially changes the artifact. Otherwise make a reversible visual assumption and proceed.

### Executive-deck production contract

Before opening a presentation tool, create one internal row per slide with:

`audience question | claim title | dominant object | implication | evidence status | source`

Reject a row when its title is only a topic, its dominant object is `bullets`, or its claim depends on a prior assistant-generated number that has not been independently verified. Do not reproduce the prior conversation chronologically; rebuild the story around the decision and the audience's likely objections.

When the audience is a founder or executive and no visual direction is supplied, default to a restrained editorial strategy deck:

- large conclusion headlines and substantial whitespace;
- one strong composition per slide rather than a grid of interchangeable cards;
- a limited neutral palette with one decision accent;
- direct labels, deliberate alignment, and varied slide silhouettes;
- charts only for real data, diagrams only for real mechanisms, and tables only for exact comparisons;
- no decorative stock imagery, framework logos, fake dashboards, or ornamental 2x2s.

The deck should feel authored for this decision, not populated from a presentation component library.

Run two QA passes:

1. **Decision QA:** recommendation, trade-offs, economics, evidence, non-goals, risks, and ask remain visible.
2. **Visual QA:** reading order, hierarchy, legibility, contrast, clipping, wrapping, overlap, source readability, and cross-page consistency are sound at rendered size.

Then run a mechanical density check: count audience-visible words for every primary page or slide and revise any one-pager above 250 words, normal slide above 45 words, or comparison-table slide above 90 words. Do not deliver on an assumption that the layout will make excess copy feel shorter.

For PowerPoint, QA the final exported file in this order:

1. run the presentation overflow/overlap test;
2. render every slide;
3. inspect every rendered slide at full size;
4. inspect a montage for story rhythm, density spikes, repeated templates, and visual monotony;
5. revise the source, re-export, rerun the tests, and rerender;
6. confirm that the inspected images came from the exact file being delivered.

Do not deliver a deck with known failures, unresolved placeholders, tiny citations, decorative visuals that do not carry meaning, or slides that require the presenter to explain what the audience should notice. Successful file creation is not successful presentation creation.

## 10. Completion checks

Before delivery, verify:

- Can the decision owner state the recommendation after a brief scan?
- Does the artifact explain why the recommendation wins?
- Are customer value and business or mission value visibly connected?
- Are alternatives and exclusions explicit?
- Are numbers real, sourced, correctly labeled, and internally consistent?
- Are assumptions and unknowns visible rather than smoothed over?
- Does each visual encode a useful relationship or evidence object?
- Is detailed analysis available without crowding the primary view?
- Is the requested file actually created and visually verified?
- Is the next decision, owner, or evidence gate unambiguous?
