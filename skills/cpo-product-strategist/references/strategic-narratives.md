## 1. Choose the narrative by decision

Do not force every strategy into a trend-led story. Select the narrative that explains the actual decision:

| Decision condition | Narrative spine |
|---|---|
| External change creates a new opportunity | change → local impact → tension → options → investment choice |
| A founder or expert is the capacity bottleneck | current value system → capacity ceiling → leverage models → staged escape |
| Growth has deteriorated | state/revenue bridge → constraint → causal options → evidence gates |
| A portfolio needs reallocation | roles/trajectory → concentration → options → allocation and transitions |
| A major transformation is proposed | why now → current system → target capability → pathways → economics and risks |

Use trends only when they causally alter customer behavior, capability, cost, competition, regulation, distribution, or defensibility. Trend wallpaper is not a `why now`.

## 2. Opportunity-to-investment narrative

For an opportunity or transformation deck, use this sequence when the evidence supports it:

1. **External change:** the few verified shifts that alter what is now possible or valuable.
2. **Current system:** assets, audience, relationships, economics, delivery model, and constraints in the focal business.
3. **Strategic tension:** the mechanism preventing the desired outcome. State the conflict, not merely the symptom.
4. **Option space:** materially different value and operating models created by the change and the available assets.
5. **Early strategic comparison:** where each option creates customer value and owner/company leverage.
6. **Option deep dives:** mechanism, offer, buyer, economics, time, dependencies, risks, and evidence for each.
7. **Investment comparison:** capital, time-to-launch, payback, cumulative contribution, downside, option value, and execution risk.
8. **Recommendation:** selected path, rejected alternatives, sequencing, evidence gates, and the decision or resources requested.

Do not tell the story in the order ideas appeared in the conversation. Reconstruct it around the audience's decision and likely objections.

## 3. Expert-capacity and founder-leverage cases

For a business whose value is concentrated in one person, expose the governing constraint:

`contribution ≈ paid outcomes × contribution per outcome − fixed investment`

and

`paid outcomes ≤ expert hours available ÷ expert hours required per outcome`

Then show the human consequence: revenue, client value, and continuity remain coupled to one person's time, energy, health, attention, and willingness to keep delivering. Treat this as a business-model and key-person-risk problem, not as a motivation problem.

Identify the assets that could create leverage:

- expertise, judgment patterns, methods, cases, and proprietary data;
- trusted brand, audience, referrals, and distribution;
- network of complementary experts;
- repeatable customer contexts and decision workflows;
- client data, organizational profiles, integrations, and ongoing use cases.

Do not equate an LLM imitation with a reliable digital twin. Specify task boundaries, evaluation, confidence, escalation to a human, update cadence, data rights, failure modes, and claims the product must not make.

## 4. Construct the option space

For any product, generate materially different strategic options by varying the choices that actually change the system:

- target user, buyer, job, geography, channel, or use context;
- value proposition, promise boundary, and competitive alternative;
- product, service, platform, marketplace, partnership, licensing, or internal-capability model;
- self-serve, assisted, managed, embedded, or enterprise delivery;
- transaction, subscription, usage, outcome, licensing, advertising, take-rate, hardware-plus-service, or mission-value mechanism;
- build, buy, partner, integrate, white-label, or sunset posture;
- degree of automation, human involvement, customization, ownership, and capital commitment.

Options must represent different choices and economic mechanisms, not different feature bundles or marketing descriptions.

### Expert-led capacity pattern

For founder-, creator-, advisor-, or expert-led businesses whose scarce human capacity is the pivotal constraint, generate options from distinct leverage mechanisms. A useful spectrum is:

1. **Price/selectivity leverage:** fewer, higher-value personal engagements.
2. **Process leverage:** standardized diagnostics, playbooks, or concierge delivery with limited expert review.
3. **Code/data leverage:** asynchronous product using a bounded expert profile for repeatable low-risk tasks.
4. **Augmented-expert leverage:** the expert profile plus specialized analytical roles, tools, evidence, and human escalation.
5. **Network leverage:** a council composed of several complementary real expert profiles.
6. **Embedded-client leverage:** combine external expertise with digitized client context or leadership profiles, leaving a governed capability that continues after the engagement.

These expert-capacity mechanisms are design spaces, not a mandatory ladder or a default for other product archetypes. Combine them only when the combination has a coherent buyer, outcome, operating model, and economic mechanism. Include the current model or do-nothing path as a baseline when it is viable.

Do not confuse alternatives, tiers, and stages:

- an **alternative** is a different end-state investment choice;
- a **tier** serves a different job, risk level, or willingness to pay inside one coherent model;
- a **stage** is an evidence-gated step that preserves or unlocks a later option.

In an expert-led case, human premium work may be the escalation and trust layer for a digital product rather than a competing option. A lightweight advisor may be an acquisition or diagnostic tier rather than the core profit engine. A client-embedded capability may be a separate enterprise model with a different buyer, sales motion, governance, and economics. Show these relationships explicitly before comparing options.

When several models share assets or infrastructure, compare the end states and also show the migration path. Prefer a first move that tests the hardest assumption, earns revenue or evidence, and preserves valuable later options without prematurely funding the full platform.

Do not equate `simplest product` with `best first move`. Separate product ambition from test implementation. A manual, concierge, or wizard-of-oz version can test the buyer, outcome, orchestration, trust, and willingness to pay of an advanced council or embedded-capability model before software is built. Select the first move by decision value and learning per unit of capital—not by familiarity or implementation ease alone.

For every option define:

- target user, decision owner, economic buyer/payer, champion, approver or blocker, beneficiary, job, and costly decision;
- buying context, trigger, frequency, risk level, current alternative, and explicit non-target audience;
- promised outcome and explicit promise boundary;
- why this model is better than the buyer's current alternative;
- delivery actors and the roles of automation, human judgment, partners, platform participants, physical operations, and customer or internal context as applicable;
- acquisition, delivery, renewal, and escalation flow;
- revenue/value metric and recurrence mechanism;
- data, evaluation, governance, privacy, and IP requirements;
- what must be true for quality and trust.

For AI products that use synthetic roles, named expert profiles, or customer context, distinguish three sources of perspective:

- **functional skills or synthetic roles** apply an explicit analytical lens but must not impersonate a real person;
- **licensed expert profiles** represent a named person's bounded judgment and require consent, attribution, evaluation, update, IP, royalty, and liability rules;
- **client profiles and data** add organizational context and require access, privacy, governance, freshness, and deletion controls.

In a multi-agent or council product, value must come from complementary perspectives, structured disagreement, evidence, synthesis, and escalation—not from displaying many agents. For a client-embedded capability, test retained value beyond the session: decision memory and rationale, shared assumptions, contradiction detection, pre-mortems, scenario refresh, action/evidence tracking, leadership continuity, and governed re-use. Treat each as a value hypothesis rather than an automatic feature list.

### Audience architecture

Do not describe an option as `for entrepreneurs`, `for companies`, or `for leaders` without identifying the purchase situation. Segment primarily by job, decision stakes, recurrence, organizational complexity, available context, and willingness to pay; use company size or title only when it changes these mechanisms.

For every option show:

| Role | Required answer |
|---|---|
| User | Who directly interacts with the product or participates in the session? |
| Decision owner | Who owns the business decision and its consequences? |
| Economic buyer/payer | Whose budget pays and what value justifies the spend? |
| Champion | Who brings the product into the organization and drives adoption? |
| Approver/blocker | Who can stop the purchase: procurement, security, legal, IT, board, or another role? |
| Beneficiary | Who gains even if they do not use or buy it directly? |
| Trigger | What event or costly uncertainty makes the customer act now? |
| Non-target | Who should not buy this option and why? |

Audience architecture differs by archetype and model. In a self-serve consumer product, user, decision maker, and payer may be the same person. In a family, education, healthcare, or public product, payer, user, approver, and beneficiary may differ. A marketplace has distinct actors on each side. A team product may be bought by a manager and used by contributors. Enterprise software or services usually require a champion, economic buyer, multiple users, and security, legal, IT, finance, or procurement approval. Do not reuse one generic persona across options or product archetypes.

## 5. Compare twice for different purposes

Use two comparisons because they answer different questions.

### Early strategic/value comparison

Answer `Why is this option interesting?` Compare:

- customer outcome magnitude and decision importance;
- willingness-to-pay logic;
- fit with existing assets and distribution;
- leverage of scarce expert time;
- repeatability and recurrence;
- differentiation, learning, and defensibility;
- residual key-person dependency.

Keep this comparison qualitative when product-specific evidence is absent. Explain each rating; do not hide judgment in a decorative score.

Show the two lenses separately:

- **customer lens:** outcome magnitude, decision quality, speed, continuity, trust, and switching from the current alternative;
- **owner/company lens:** expert-time leverage, revenue quality, strategic fit, defensibility, recurrence, and residual key-person risk.

Do not let an easy but low-value model win merely because it is easy. Preserve the upside and learning value of a more differentiated model in the comparison.

### Late investment comparison

Answer `Where should we invest?` Compare:

- build and launch investment;
- time and dependencies to credible launch;
- expert and operating capacity required;
- contribution margin and cash profile;
- break-even and payback conditions;
- downside capital at risk;
- cumulative three- to five-year contribution or NPV when inputs support it;
- execution, adoption, quality, legal, and reputational risk;
- option value and strategic learning created.
- decision-relevant learning obtained per unit of capital and time.

Do not collapse both comparisons into one weighted total unless the decision owner approves explicit weights. Show confidence and missing inputs next to each conclusion.

## 6. Build each option's business case

Use one or two slides per serious option, or one compact module per option in a written artifact.

Use two slides when the value/delivery mechanism and the investment case each require a different dominant visual. Do not force a four- or five-model decision into an arbitrary eight-slide cap. `Short` means low cognitive load, disciplined speaking time, and one decision question per slide—not the smallest possible slide count. A sparse 14–18 slide deck can be substantially shorter to understand than eight dense slides.

**Mechanism view:** buyer and job → experience/delivery system → customer outcome → revenue/mission mechanism → retained asset or learning loop.

**Investment view:**

- one-time build, research, legal, data, and go-to-market cost;
- fixed, variable, step-fixed, compute/tool, human-review, and support cost;
- launch gates and supported timing range;
- price/contract logic and units sold or accounts needed;
- contribution per unit/account and expert hours consumed;
- break-even volume and cash trough;
- downside/base/upside only from explicit drivers;
- cumulative contribution by year when supported;
- dominant sensitivity, risks, and evidence that would change the case.

Every number needs a source state: supplied, verified, estimate from stated inputs, assumption to test, or unknown. Research market and cost benchmarks when current facts are decision-relevant. Never convert an earlier assistant illustration into a forecast.

## 7. Use visuals as reasoning objects

Prefer these objects when they match the evidence:

| Story beat | Useful visual |
|---|---|
| External change | convergence of two to four causal forces with direct implications |
| Current system | value flow, asset map, or capacity model |
| Strategic tension | bottleneck diagram or supplied-data capacity/revenue curve |
| Option space | leverage spectrum or operating-model map |
| Audience fit | buyer/user/payer map or job-by-model matrix |
| Early comparison | compact criteria table with evidence/confidence labels |
| Option mechanism | annotated flow or value-and-revenue loop |
| Option economics | investment/payback timeline, sensitivity, or transparent scenario table |
| Final comparison | investment frontier, scenario table, or risk-return map |
| Recommendation | staged pathway with evidence gates and preserved options |

Use a schematic only when clearly labeled qualitative. Do not present a drawn curve, bubble size, axis position, or timeline as measured data unless it is.

## 8. Compress to one or two pages

Use two pages by default when both strategic logic and investment logic matter.

**Page 1 — Why change and what could win**

- verified external shifts and direct implication;
- current asset/constraint system;
- the pivotal tension;
- option landscape;
- target audience and purchase trigger for every option;
- early customer-value and owner/company-leverage comparison.

**Page 2 — Where to invest**

- compact business case for each shortlisted option;
- investment/economics comparison;
- recommendation and rejected paths;
- staged sequence, evidence gates, major risks, and the exact next decision.

Use a one-page version only when at most three options and a small number of economic drivers remain legible. Move calculations and source detail to an appendix rather than shrinking type.

## 9. Quality gate

Before delivery, verify:

- every trend changes the local decision rather than decorating the opening;
- the current system includes both distinctive assets and real constraints;
- the tension states a causal conflict and human/business consequence;
- options represent different value and operating models, not feature bundles;
- every option names its user, decision owner, payer, purchase trigger, and non-target rather than relying on a generic audience;
- customer value and owner/company leverage are both visible;
- the early comparison and investment comparison answer different questions;
- each shortlisted option has an explicit mechanism and economic model;
- timing, payback, and long-horizon value use supported inputs and transparent uncertainty;
- the recommendation explains sequencing, what not to fund, and what evidence could reverse it;
- the first step tests the highest-value strategic uncertainty rather than defaulting to the most familiar or easiest product;
- the artifact can be understood visually without reading a report aloud.
